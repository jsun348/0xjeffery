The Practice Room: Music Theory Drills That Run in the Page

A page that is the exercise rather than an explanation of it. First drill: the note circle — count the semitones between two notes, or name the note a given distance away. More sections to follow.

Music theory is not knowledge, it is latency. Everyone who has spent an evening with a chord chart knows what a minor third is; almost nobody can say, without counting on their fingers, what sits a minor third above A♭. The gap between those two states is not understanding. It is repetition — and repetition wants a drill, not a paragraph.

So this page is the drill. It runs entirely in the browser, holds no account, keeps no score past the tab, and works on a phone with one thumb. It will grow a section at a time as I hit the next thing I am slow at; the note circle is the first.

The note circle

Twelve pitch classes, evenly spaced, wrapping at the octave. Every interval question is the same question asked from one of two ends: the distance between two points, or the point a given distance away. Both directions are below — the tabs switch between them.

 

Exhibit 1. The twelve pitch classes as a clock face. Every interval question is the same question asked twice — a distance between two points, or the point a distance away. Enharmonics are accepted: if the pitch is right, the spelling is yours.

A few decisions worth stating, since they change what the drill teaches:

It grades by pitch class, not by spelling. Ask for the note seven semitones above A and the answer is E; F♭ is accepted, because it is the same key. That is a deliberate narrowing. Whether a pitch should be written G♯ or A♭ is a question about the key you are in and the line you are writing, and it deserves its own drill later. Conflating it with distance arithmetic just makes both slower to learn.

The interval is always read upwards from the first note. “A ↑ B♭” is 1, not 11. Without that convention half the answers are ambiguous, and ambiguity in a speed drill teaches hesitation.

Wrong answers stop the clock. A correct answer rolls straight into the next question; a wrong one freezes, lights the arc on the circle, and waits for you. The point of seeing the arc is to attach a shape to the number — twelve is the whole face, six is straight across, seven is the one you will use most.

At a desk, the keyboard works: digits for the intervals, with - and = carrying 10 and 11 where they sit on the number row; letters for the notes, with - and = again toggling flat and sharp before you name one.

The accidental problem

Getting ♯ and ♭ onto a phone is the whole interface question for the second drill, and it has no clean answer. A text field means summoning the keyboard, hunting for a symbol that is not on the primary plane, and losing a second per question — which defeats the exercise.

What is here instead: the accidental is a sticky modifier, not a character. Tap ♭ or ♯, then tap a letter, and the answer submits on the letter. Naturals need no modifier at all, so the common case is one tap and the worst case is two. The modifier clears itself after every answer, because a mode you can get stuck in silently is worse than an extra tap.

I do not think this is obviously right. The alternative worth trying is a twelve-key chromatic strip laid out like a keyboard octave — one tap always, at the cost of asking you to read a piano rather than a name. If the two-tap version starts to feel like the bottleneck rather than the recall, that is the version that replaces it.

What comes next

The list, roughly in the order I expect to need them:

  • Intervals by name — major third, perfect fourth, tritone — mapped onto the semitone counts above, in both directions.
  • Key signatures — how many sharps or flats, and which ones, in what order.
  • Chord spelling — triads and sevenths from a root, then inversions.
  • Spelling proper — the G♯-or-A♭ question the first drill deliberately sets aside.

Each will be a section on this page rather than a post of its own. A practice room with the equipment in one place beats four rooms with one machine each.
